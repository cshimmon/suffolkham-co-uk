Emergency communications

RAYNET in Suffolk

RAYNET — the Radio Amateurs' Emergency Network — is a volunteer organisation that provides emergency communications support to the emergency services, local authorities and other agencies when normal communications fail. Suffolk has an active RAYNET group and welcomes new members.

About RAYNET

What does RAYNET do?

Amateur radio operators in RAYNET provide a resilient, independent communications capability that functions when commercial infrastructure fails — during floods, power cuts, major incidents or large public events.

Emergency support

RAYNET groups are registered with local authorities and emergency services as an emergency communications resource. Members can be called out to support police, ambulance, fire, local councils and voluntary organisations when normal comms are disrupted.

Public events

As well as emergency activations, RAYNET supports planned public events — community events, marathons, cycle rides, walking events — where reliable communications help organisers manage participants safely across wide areas.

Training and exercises

RAYNET members train regularly, practising message passing, net control procedures, and operating from portable setups. This keeps skills sharp so that when activation comes, the group can operate effectively under pressure.

Independence from infrastructure

The key advantage of amateur radio in emergencies is infrastructure independence. A RAYNET station needs only a radio, antenna and a power source — it doesn't rely on mobile networks, internet, or mains power. Battery and solar-powered setups can operate for days.

Local group

East Suffolk RAYNET

East Suffolk RAYNET covers the East Suffolk area and is affiliated to the national RAYNET-UK organisation. The group welcomes licensed amateur radio operators who want to use their skills in public service.

How to get involved

  • 1 Hold a valid UK amateur radio licence. Any licence level — Foundation, Intermediate or Full — qualifies you to join RAYNET, though higher licence levels give access to more power and frequencies for emergency use.
  • 2 Contact East Suffolk RAYNET. Introduce yourself via the website or at a club meeting. The group will explain what's expected of members and invite you to training sessions.
  • 3 Attend training and exercises. Regular exercises test and develop the group's skills. Participation is key — the more you train, the more effective you'll be when called upon.
  • 4 Build a portable capability. A handheld radio and a simple antenna are enough to start. Over time you might build a more capable portable station — a mobile transceiver, battery pack, and wire antenna that fits in the boot of a car.
Emergency communications

IARU emergency frequencies

The International Amateur Radio Union (IARU) has designated a set of frequencies for emergency and disaster communications. These are kept clear of general QSOs during normal operation and are monitored during major incidents.

These frequencies are not for casual use. Do not conduct routine QSOs on IARU emergency frequencies. They are reserved for genuine emergency and disaster communications. In an emergency, you may use any frequency necessary to call for help — but do not simulate emergencies.

MAYDAY on amateur radio

In a genuine life-threatening emergency, use any means available. On amateur radio you may call MAYDAY on any frequency where you believe you will be heard. Give your callsign, position, nature of emergency, and number of people involved. The calling frequencies (145.500 MHz, 433.500 MHz) are the best starting point on VHF/UHF.

Phonetic alphabet

Clear communication under stress requires the NATO phonetic alphabet: Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliet, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu. Callsigns are always passed phonetically during emergency traffic.

Practical guidance

Kit for emergency communications

You don't need specialist equipment to contribute to RAYNET. A handheld radio gets you started; a more capable portable station greatly extends what you can do.

Start with a handheld

Any dual-band (2m/70cm) handheld covers the local VHF/UHF frequencies used by RAYNET. A rubber-duck antenna works for short distances; adding an external antenna dramatically improves range. Keep a spare battery charged and ready.

Mobile transceiver

A 2m/70cm mobile transceiver (25–50 W) in a vehicle gives much better range than a handheld. With a rooftop antenna and a leisure battery or car battery, you can operate independently of mains power for many hours.

HF capability

For longer-range coordination — county to county, or national — HF is essential. An HF transceiver with a wire antenna covers hundreds of miles on 80m and 40m. This requires a Full licence. RAYNET exercises often include HF nets.

Power independence

A 20–100 Ah leisure battery paired with a basic solar panel keeps a station running for days. Ensure your power connections are fused and your battery is tested regularly — a flat battery at deployment is a common and avoidable failure.

Antenna options

A simple wire dipole or end-fed half-wave antenna can be erected in minutes with fishing poles or tossed over a tree branch. Keep a selection of lengths cut for 2m, 40m and 80m. Wire, connectors and a few metres of coax take up little space in a go-bag.

Logs and message forms

Emergency communications requires proper logging. Keep a paper log (electronics fail). Use standardised message forms where possible — RAYNET UK provides templates. Accurate records matter when handing over to a new operator or briefing agency liaisons.

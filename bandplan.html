UK Band Plan

A quick reference to the UK amateur radio band plan for the bands most commonly used in Suffolk. Includes repeater offsets, calling frequencies, and CTCSS tones. Based on the RSGB band plan.

144–146 MHz

2m Band

The most popular VHF band for local FM operation in the UK. Home to numerous repeaters, the national FM calling channel, APRS and SSB activity.

New to 2m? Start by monitoring 145.500 MHz (no CTCSS needed for simplex). To access local repeater GB3PO, set your radio to 145.650 MHz receive, 145.050 MHz transmit, with CTCSS tone 110.9 Hz. Most modern radios handle this automatically if you enter the output frequency and set the offset to −600 kHz.

430–440 MHz

70cm Band

The main UHF band for amateur use in the UK. Supports FM, DMR, D-STAR, SSB and data. Two different repeater channel plans operate here — RB (traditional FM) and RU (wide-area/digital).

RB Repeater Offset+1.6 MHz
RU Repeater Offset−9 MHz
Standard CTCSS110.9 Hz
Channel Spacing25 kHz (RB)
FM Calling433.500 MHz
Two repeater types on 70cm. RB repeaters have outputs in the 433 MHz region — input is 1.6 MHz above the output (e.g. output 433.350, input 434.950). RU repeaters (often digital/wide-area) have outputs around 439 MHz — input is 9 MHz below the output (e.g. output 439.400, input 430.400). Always check the repeaters page for local frequencies.

70–70.5 MHz

4m Band

A narrow 500 kHz allocation unique to a handful of countries. Good for sporadic-E propagation in summer and local simplex use. The band is less crowded than 2m and can give surprising range.

Sporadic-E (Es). The 4m band can produce dramatic long-distance contacts via sporadic-E propagation, typically May–August. When Es opens, signals from mainland Europe can boom in at S9+. Check DX cluster spots for 4m Es openings. The FDARS Sunday net includes a 4m leg — see the nets page.
50–52 MHz

6m Band

Known as the "magic band" for its ability to produce unexpected long-distance propagation via sporadic-E, meteor scatter and auroral reflection. The 6m allocation in the UK is 50–52 MHz.

FM Repeater Offset+500 kHz
FM Calling51.500 MHz
SSB Calling50.150 MHz
Note on the 6m repeater offset. Unlike 2m where the input is below the output, 6m UK repeaters have their input above the output by 500 kHz. So your radio transmits 500 kHz higher than it receives — the opposite of 2m. This catches many beginners out. Example: GB3EF outputs on 51.720 MHz, input on 51.220 MHz (your radio transmits on 51.220, receives on 51.720 — set your offset to +500 kHz from input perspective, or simply set TX = 51.220, RX = 51.720).

IUAR Emergency Frequencies

These frequencies are monitored during emergencies and are kept clear of general QSOs during normal operation:

  • 3.760 MHz — 80m (European)
  • 7.110 MHz — 40m (Region 1)
  • 14.300 MHz — 20m (worldwide)
  • 18.160 MHz — 17m
  • 21.360 MHz — 15m

Getting on HF

HF requires a Full licence in the UK. If you're not there yet, you can still listen — all of the HF bands are fascinating to receive. A simple wire antenna (even a random length fed via an ATU) can produce excellent results on 80m and 40m. Check out the getting started page for UK licensing steps.

Reference

UK CTCSS tones

CTCSS (Continuous Tone-Coded Squelch System) sub-audible tones are used by most UK FM repeaters to prevent interference from distant stations opening the squelch. You must transmit the correct tone for the repeater to activate.

110.9 Hz — most common in the UK

The majority of UK amateur FM repeaters use 110.9 Hz as their access tone. This includes all the SARG repeaters in Suffolk. If in doubt, try 110.9 Hz first.

Other tones in use locally

Some repeaters in the region use different tones: GB3CE (Colchester) and GB3CL (Clacton) both use 67.0 Hz. MB7IBW uses 94.8 Hz. Always check the repeaters page for the correct tone.

Setting CTCSS on your radio

Look for CTCSS, TONE, or T-CTSS in your radio's channel settings. Some radios call it a "subtone" or "PL tone". Set it to the transmit tone only — you generally don't need to decode CTCSS on receive for repeater use.

No CTCSS for simplex

On simplex frequencies like 145.500 MHz, CTCSS is not normally used — just transmit without a tone. Enabling CTCSS on simplex can prevent you hearing stations who aren't using a tone.
